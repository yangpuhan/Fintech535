The Nightly Wheel

An algorithm is a function that takes inputs and returns open orders. Every weeknight a GitHub Action pulls AAPL from LSEG and a credit spread from FRED, reads the book Sanji wrote, and writes the next session's orders to open_orders.json. The rule is 1.4's wheel with two changes: no new option across an earnings report, and no new put while corporate credit is getting worse.

    01The algorithm

    Decided on the last close, every night, from the book Sanji wrote (until he writes one, the opening book: $100,000 cash).

    Wheel
    Cash: sell cash-secured puts on the first monthly expiry at least 21 days out, at the strike nearest −0.30 delta, as many as the cash secures. Assigned: own the shares; cost basis = strike − premium. Shares: sell one call per 100 shares, same expiry rule, nearest +0.30 delta among strikes at or above the basis. Called away: back to cash. Unused cash, including what secures a put, earns T-bill interest.
    Profit-take
    Buy a short back once it costs half what it was sold for; sell the next one the night after.
    Earnings
    No new put or call whose life spans Apple's next report date. Reports gap the stock overnight; in 1.4's wheel the calls held through one lost $25,103.
    Credit
    No new put while the Baa–Treasury spread (Moody's Baa corporate yield less the 10-year Treasury, from FRED) is above its 200-day average. A widening spread means lenders are getting nervous about companies, which tends to come before equity selloffs. Calls are not paused — a covered call only lowers risk.
    Delta
    Computed from each contract's last closing quote (Black-Scholes, 1.3/1.4's greeks.py), because Sanji does not accept delta:30 yet. The chosen strike goes to Sanji as a number, with a dated expiry.

    02Why this algorithm

    • The wheel was the semester's best result. In 1.4 it beat buying the stock on risk-adjusted return, and the reason was timing: the put buys AAPL only after a fall, and the basis rule holds it through the rebound.
    • Its losses had a pattern. 1.2's worst week was a call sold the day before Apple reported; across 1.4's wheel, the calls held through a report lost $25,103, the worst of any group of its trades.
    • What I kept, and what I didn't. On 1.4's engine and data carried to November 2026 I tested eight additions — the two rules above, a trend/volatility switch, an implied-vs-realised vol filter, an open-interest strike, a VIX pause, volatility sizing and a defensive roll — each on 1.4's original window and again on the data since October 2025. The earnings blackout made the clearest difference; the credit pause added a smaller gain in risk-adjusted return (Sharpe 0.79 → 0.83). The rest hurt or did little, and were left out.

    03How it works

    Five steps, two machines. GitHub runs the strategy; the course server, Luffy, runs Sanji. The strategy never talks to a broker: it reads the book and writes orders.

    One order, in Sanji

    …
    • SHT sells to open; expiry and strike name the exact contract, so Sanji needs no chain to find it.
    • lmt_prc: "mid" — the midpoint of that option's quote at the open.
    • The id carries action, contract and session, so a second run the same night writes the same id and Sanji fills it once.
    • Profit-takes go in as BTC limits at half the entry price.

    Safeguards

    • Late runs. GitHub starts scheduled runs hours late. If the last close is not the session before the one being planned, Sanji has not updated the book yet, so no new orders are written.
    • Unknown book. Sanji has not published a positions.json yet; if its shape is not one the script understands, it writes no new orders rather than guess.
    • Missing data. If FRED cannot be read, no new put that night.
    • No look-ahead. Cutting every price series at a date and re-running gives the same trades up to that date (1.4's test).
