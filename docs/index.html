Ten weeks short the wrong option

Long 100 AAPL, short one weekly call, held to expiry, every week, no discretion. The rule made money. It made less money than doing nothing, and the gap is the whole lesson.

01The rule

Everything below is mechanical. There is no judgement anywhere in the loop — given the same tape it produces the same blotter, and the strike is picked by arithmetic rather than by opinion.

Entry
First trading session of the expiry week, at the 14:00 UTC bar (10:00 ET). One hour into the session: late enough that the opening auction has cleared and the call has a two-sided quote, early enough to be a decision rather than a closing mark dressed up as a fill. "Monday" names the first session, not a date — 7 September is Labor Day, so that week enters Tuesday the 8th.
Stock
If flat, BUY 100 shares at the print on that bar. If last week's call expired worthless the shares are still here, so nothing is bought and the basis carries. Two of the ten weeks open already long, which is why the blotter has 18 option lines and only 8 stock buys.
Strike
SELL 1 call expiring that Friday, struck at the lowest listed strike ≥ spot. Nearest out-of-the-money; at-the-money only if spot sits exactly on a strike. AAPL weeklies list on a $2.50 grid at this price — probed, not assumed: 328, 329, 331 and 332 do not resolve; 330.00 and 332.50 do. The grid is the binding constraint, and section 05 is mostly about what it did to this book.
Fill
Option at mid = (BID + ASK) / 2 on the order bar, as a limit. Stock at the print. No bid or no ask → no fill, skip the week. Section 06 tests this against the tape rather than asserting it.
Combo
The two legs go on together or not at all. If the call cannot be priced while the book is flat, the stock is not bought either. Buying 100 shares with no call against them is a different strategy, and a backtest that quietly does it is lying about what it tested. Already long and the call cannot be priced: hold the shares uncovered and write nothing. This branch never fired — all ten weeks had a two-sided quote.
Expiry
Wait through it. No rolls, no buy-to-close. Settlement is the last stock print of the expiry session.
settle ≤ K → EXPIRE. Short call to zero, keep the shares, keep the premium, cash unchanged; next week just writes again.
settle > K → ASSIGNED. Deliver 100 shares at K, cash up by 100 × K, flat; next week starts the combo over. Ties go to the writer: settle exactly equal to K expires worthless, which is what the contract says.
Cash
Cash moves on blotter events only — stock bought, premium collected, expiry at zero, assignment at the strike. Marks move NAV and never move cash.

03NAV and Reg T, bar by bar

Net liquidation value against the two requirements it has to clear. Reg T, not portfolio margin: the arithmetic is fixed percentages of the stock's market value, so it is computable without a broker's risk engine — and a book that lives inside Reg T also lives inside PM, not the reverse.

Hover for every field at that bar. Vertical ticks along the axis mark blotter events. The comb pattern is real and worth a second look: the instant an assignment leaves the book flat, LMV goes to zero, both requirements go to zero with it, and available funds snaps all the way up to NAV. The requirement only exists while the stock is held — which is the only reason an account this size could carry of stock for ten weeks without ever taking a call.

The arithmetic

LMV    = shares × stock mark
NAV    = cash + stock MV + option MV
        (the short call is negative)
Initial = 50% × stock LMV   + $0 for the covered call
Maint  = 25% × stock LMV
Avail  = NAV − initial
Excess = NAV − maintenance
Why the covered call adds $0. The short call is covered by the 100 shares for its whole life — the shares are the deliverable, so there is no scenario where the position owes more stock than it holds. That is the entire reason this structure is computable by hand. A naked short call is not: its requirement is a percentage of the underlying plus out-of-the-money adjustments, and it moves every bar.

06Is a fill at the mid honest?

The book assumed every call was sold at the midpoint. That assumption is free to make and expensive to be wrong about, so here it is against the prints on the same bars. Three separate questions, and they are not the same question.

Where the print landed inside its own quote, in half-spreads. −1 is the bid, 0 the mid, +1 the offer. The end bars collect everything beyond ±1.5: of prints sit outside the quote entirely, which is what an hourly bar does to you — TRDPRC_1 is the last trade of the hour and BID/ASK are the quote at the end of it, so any bar where the market moved leaves the two describing different moments.

A high R² here is nearly free, and I do not want credit for it. Both series are dominated by moneyness — a deep in-the-money call at $40 and a wing at $0.05 will line up on any axis, so the regression mostly proves that expensive options are expensive. It is necessary and it is not sufficient. The two numbers that actually license the assumption are underneath it: how often the print lands inside the quote, and whether the midpoint is biased against a seller. 07What happened

Each week decomposed. The stock leg marks open-to-settle; the call leg keeps its premium and pays out its intrinsic value at expiry. The two columns add to the week, and the weeks add to the NAV change exactly.

The headline
Why the strike rule, and what it cost
Nearest out-of-the-money is the most aggressive defensible rule: it collects the most premium of any OTM strike and gives up the most upside. I chose it because it is unambiguous — "lowest listed strike ≥ spot" needs no parameter, no delta target, no volatility input, and cannot be tuned after the fact to make the backtest look better. That property is worth more in a graded exercise than a cleverer rule with a knob on it.
What I did not appreciate until the blotter existed is that the strike grid, not the rule, set the risk. AAPL weeklies list every $2.50, and AAPL traded near $310, so "nearest OTM" is arithmetically pinned between 0.1% and 0.8% above spot — on average. An average AAPL week moves several times that. The rule did not choose to sell a call with a high chance of assignment; the grid chose it, and the rule had no other option to pick. Eight of ten weeks were assigned. On a $15 stock with $0.50 strikes the same rule would be four times as far out in percentage terms and a completely different strategy wearing the same name.
Where theory met tape
Theory held on the microstructure and broke on the payoff. The fill assumption survived contact with the tape better than I expected — near-the-money AAPL weeklies are genuinely liquid, the mid is unbiased at the median, and all ten of the book's own orders were on contracts that traded thousands of times that hour. If this backtest is wrong, it is not wrong because of the fills.
It is wrong-footed by the part no fill model can rescue. The premium line looks wonderful in isolation: collected over ten weeks, never a losing week on the option at the time of sale. Then the settlement column arrives. was handed straight back as intrinsic value on the eight assignments, so the option leg lost net. The stock leg made , and it made it almost entirely in the weeks the call was capping it.
The clearest single week is 11 September: bought at 315.99, wrote the 317.50 for $3.85, and AAPL settled at 332.22. The stock leg made $1,623. The call gave back $1,472 of it. Net $536 on a week where simply holding the shares made three times that. And the mirror image is 31 July, which is the week this book learned the most.
The rule bought at 338.21 on Monday 27 July and wrote the 340 call for $6.02 — by some distance the largest premium of the ten, against a median of $3.34. It had no idea why. Apple reported at 16:31 on Thursday 30 July (LSEG TR.EPSActReportDate), after the close, one session before that call expired. The rule had sold a weekly option straddling an earnings report without any term in it that knows what an earnings report is. The market knew: the doubled premium was the market saying so, and the rule collected it as though it were free money.
It was not free money. AAPL settled at 309.05 and the call expired worthless — one of only two weeks it did, and the best single result the option leg ever had — while the shares underneath it lost $2,916. The $602.50 premium covered 21% of that. You keep the premium exactly when you least want to be keeping it, and on the one week the option leg looked brilliant the combined position had its worst result of the run.
The one thing a reader should not skip
The strategy's return is not what it looks like. over ten weeks is a perfectly respectable number, and it is entirely an artefact of the denominator I chose. The book runs a Reg T debit the whole time it is long — at its peak it holds of stock against of opening equity, so the equity return is levered about gross. The buy-and-hold comparison at the top is run on exactly the same money over exactly the same bars and is therefore the honest one. The covered call underperformed doing nothing by , and that is before a dollar of financing cost.
What I would change
1 — Charge the debit. This book never pays interest on its margin loan. It carried an average debit of for ; at that is about , or of the reported profit. It is not large enough to change the conclusion, which is exactly why it should be in the ledger rather than in a caption — the cases where it matters are the cases where you would not notice.
2 — Let the strike rule see the week it is selling into. "Nearest OTM" is indifferent to whether the week is quiet or contains an earnings report, and section 07 shows what that costs. Two fixes, both still mechanical: key the strike to the week's own implied move — the first strike at least one expected move above spot, which would have pushed 27 July out past 340 — or read the earnings calendar and stand down the week that contains one. Neither needs discretion; both need an input the current rule does not have. The second is cheaper and I would do it first.
3 — Model the pin, and model early exercise. Ties go to the writer here, and settlement is the last hourly print rather than the official close. Neither bit me in these ten weeks — no settle landed within a few cents of a strike — but both are places where this backtest would quietly award itself the good outcome. An American call on a non-dividend week is rarely exercised early; "rarely" is doing work in that sentence that a real book cannot rely on.
4 — Stop calling one name a result. Ten weeks, one underlying, in a tape that went up 6.3%. This is a correctly-implemented strategy observed across a single regime. It tells you what a covered call does in a rally, which is lose to the stock, and it tells you almost nothing about what it does anywhere else.

08Two notes on the data

The RIC appendix is wrong about the day. The brief says the day is not zero-padded, and offers AAPLH72620500.U^H26 as an example. It does not resolve; AAPLH072633000.U^H26 does. The brief's one sample that does resolve has a two-digit day, so it cannot distinguish the conventions. Two Fridays in this window have single-digit days — 7 August and 4 September — so following the appendix literally would have silently dropped two of the ten weeks. Assignment 1.1 found the same class of bug in the chain suffix for puts. Constructed RICs fail quietly, which is what makes them dangerous.
LOW_1 on the hourly stock tape is not the low. The 19:00 UTC bar on 10 July reports a low of 280.11 on a day AAPL never traded below 312, and the 13:00 bar does the same on the way in. They are stray prints in the consolidated low, not the stock. Nothing in the book touches LOW_1 — fills are at TRDPRC_1 and marks are mids — but the first version of the fetcher banded its strike requests off it and asked for three times as many contracts as it needed, most of them 20% away from spot.
